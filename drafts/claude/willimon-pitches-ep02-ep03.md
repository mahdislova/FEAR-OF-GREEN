# Five Willimon pitches: Episodes 2 and 3

Beau Willimon's rule: politics is boring, power isn't. Power lives in intimate relationships and changes hands in quiet rooms, through favours, signatures, silences and gifts. Every scene below is a transaction: someone walks out holding more than they walked in with, and it pushes the season forward. **All five are pitches.** Each is logic-checked against canon.

---

## 1. The Duke's word (Ep 2, the vestry before the funeral)

**The scene.** Before the service, Eldgard finds the Duke alone in the vestry. No small talk between old friends. Eldgard asks for one thing: when the Lords debate the eastern failures, the Duke stands up and says it's a fault in the lines, temporary, being mended. The Duke has buried a wife; he knows what it costs a man to ask for something at his son's funeral. He says yes.

> **ELDGARD:** I'm not asking you to lie.
> **DUKE:** No. You're asking me to be wrong in public, so you don't have to be.
> **ELDGARD:** Will you?
> **DUKE:** For Jamie. Not for the light.

**The turn.** Two hours later, at the wake (scene 8), the same Duke asks Hector privately how many days of coal his sister's house will need. Hector reads the hour of the question. **We now know what Hector doesn't yet: the Duke has promised Eldgard to say the opposite in public.**

**What it moves forward.** It loads a gun for Episode 4. When the Duke vouches for the Hardings at the hearing, Hector has the coal order in his pocket. One question destroys the most respected witness the Hardings have: "Your Grace, how many days did you tell your sister to lay in?" Lambrick's attack in Parliament stops being only numbers; he breaks a friendship in public.

**Power shift.** Eldgard gains a witness. Hector, without knowing it yet, gains the means to turn him.

**Logic check.** Canon has the Duke expecting the light to fail (his coal question in scene 8). An old friend who privately hedges while publicly vouching out of loyalty is entirely in character. Eldgard "controlling the narrative" is canon.

## 2. Grise sells Josephine the sister (Ep 2, scene 16)

**The scene.** Josephine comes to Grise with her fee: someone close enough to Nathaniel to tell her where he goes. In canon Grise picks Alice afterwards. **Here she picks her in front of Josephine, and names her.**

> **GRISE:** I've a girl. New. Hungry. Her sister ran off and left her. *(a beat)* Bounds.
> **JOSEPHINE:** *(perfectly still)* No.
> **GRISE:** She'll never suspect the woman paying her. And if she ever learns what you did on that bridge, you'll want her where you can see her.
> **JOSEPHINE:** You're selling me my own risk.
> **GRISE:** I'm selling you the only person in London who'd look for you. Better she looks at your cuffs than your past.

Josephine pays. Grise has just made her complicit twice: in the sister's death, and in the sister's employment.

**What it moves forward.** It makes every Josephine–Alice scene unbearable on purpose. When Alice fastens Josephine's cuff buttons in Episode 4, Josephine knows exactly whose sister's fingers are on her wrist. The Episode 8 moment when Alice stops at two buttons becomes the moment Josephine's bet comes due. And Grise now holds Josephine as well as Alice, which is why Josephine has to come back to her in Episode 6 instead of going anywhere else.

**Power shift.** Grise gains a hold on the most powerful woman in London.

**Logic check.** Grise sold Josephine the rendezvous, so she knows Josephine went to the bridge. Josephine is ex-Vykra and thinks in exactly these terms: control is protection. It doesn't change who Alice works for; it only makes Josephine aware of who she's hiring.

## 3. Eldgard names the monster (Ep 3, the night after the market)

**The scene.** Nixon comes to the study to report the market killings: bodies on spikes of ice, Lambrick's men and some civilians. Eldgard listens without interrupting. Then he takes a sheet of the family's paper and drafts the Guard's public statement himself. He reads it back to Nixon once.

> **ELDGARD:** "The Frost Killer." Say it.
> **NIXON:** We don't know it's the same—
> **ELDGARD:** The frost in my son's study. The frost at the market. Londoners need one name for what they're afraid of, Captain, or they'll find their own. *(sliding the sheet across)* Sign it as the officer in charge.

Nixon signs. It's the first time he puts his name to something he doesn't know is true.

**What it moves forward.** It's canon that London names a Frost Killer and blames Jamie's death on it. This makes that an **act**, not a rumour. Eldgard turns the escaped child into a public enemy, so the whole city hunts her for him. It buries the ornament inside a murder story, and it points his own son's investigation at a monster instead of the house. It's also Nixon's first compromise, which puts his signature under the lie he'll be asked to defend in Episode 5.

**Power shift.** Eldgard takes control of the story. Nixon loses a little of himself.

**Logic check.** Eldgard knows the frost means the offspring (he trapped it in 1873). Hunting it publicly serves the pod plan. Nixon is Eldgard's officer for family inquiries, so a signed statement is his job.

## 4. Hector pays the widows (Ep 3, the morning after the market)

**The scene.** The ice killed Lambrick's own men alongside the civilians. At first light Hector stands in Gin Lane in the snow with Ajax and a strongbox and pays every family. Not only his men's: everyone's. Cash into hands, names written down, no cameras needed: the whole street is watching. The Hardings, whose light failed over this same street, send no one. Tamara holds the ledger. She understands exactly what she's watching.

> **TAMARA:** You're paying for the Hardings' monster.
> **HECTOR:** I'm paying for the street's memory. *(counting coins into a widow's hand)* In a month they'll need coal. They'll remember who came.
> **TAMARA:** And if they ask why you came?
> **HECTOR:** Grief, my dear. It's the cheapest thing I'll ever buy.

**What it moves forward.** It's the long game behind the ending, where Lambrick wins. When the East freezes in Episode 7 and coal is the only heat, Gin Lane already knows whose name to trust. Tamara learns her father's method in this scene. It's the method she'll turn against him in Episode 10, when she opens the depots to the East for nothing: the same gesture without the price.

**Power shift.** Lambrick buys a district's loyalty for the cost of a strongbox.

**Logic check.** It's canon that Lambrick's men attacked Alice and died in the ice. Hector reading the political value of grief matches his reading of the Duke in Episode 2. Tamara keeps his books.

## 5. Nathaniel helps search the wrong street (Ep 3, the Guard post at night)

**The scene.** After the market, Nathaniel goes to find Nixon at the Guard post. He's family; he comes to help. Nixon is grateful and shows him the search map: the cordon, the streets covered, the gaps. Nathaniel studies it as an engineer and points out, helpfully, that the frost would run along the old drains toward Gin Lane. "That's where I'd look." Nixon moves twenty men to Gin Lane. Clerkenwell, where the sisters are hiding in Nathaniel's workshop, is left open.

> **NIXON:** I don't know what I'd do without you tonight.
> **NATHANIEL:** *(eyes on the map)* Neither do I.

**What it moves forward.** It's canon that Nathaniel tells Nixon his first lie in Episode 3. This makes the lie *useful*, and much worse: a betrayal disguised as service. It explains how the sisters stay hidden after the market without luck doing it. And in Episode 5, when Nixon learns Nathaniel was hiding her all along, this scene re-reads in his head. His best friend stood beside him over the map and sent his men the wrong way.

**Power shift.** Nathaniel learns he's good at this. That's the most dangerous thing he learns all season, because it's his father's skill.

**Logic check.** Nathaniel has standing as family; Nixon trusts him. He has just hidden the sisters and needs the search pointed away from Clerkenwell. It doesn't contradict anything in canon, and it fills a gap: canon never says why the Guard doesn't find them.
