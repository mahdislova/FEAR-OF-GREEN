# Inside the craft of The Rings of Power
## A practical writers’ room guide for The Green Gallows

**Research date:** 17 November 2026  
**Spoiler boundary:** Story examples stop at Season 2, Episode 7.  
**Purpose:** Borrow useful methods for building a compelling ensemble fantasy season.

> The most valuable thing to borrow is a way of making decisions: whose experience organises a sequence, what a revelation changes, why someone accepts a dangerous offer, and how an episode earns its ending.

## How to read this guide

**Reported practice** means a writer or showrunner explicitly discussed the method in an interview. **Craft interpretation** means an inference from their comments or the finished show. **Our adaptation** means an exercise or tool proposed here; it is not their documented internal process.

“Peak TV” is a judgment, not a reproducible formula. Strong intentions do not guarantee successful execution. We can admire particular achievements and still test their methods critically. Public interviews provide partial accounts, often given during promotion; they are not access to the room’s boards, complete outlines, or private disagreements.

This is a development guide, not an audit or revision of the current Green Gallows files. All original examples below are hypothetical and establish no canon.

## 1. Build the season together before treating episodes as separate assignments

**Reported practice.** Hutchison describes collective season and episode breaking, followed by collaborative drafting on the first-season finale. A credited episode writer was working from shared construction, rather than independently inventing one chapter. [S1]

**Our adaptation.** Before detailed scene writing, make a shared season map containing:

| Track | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Character | Starting belief, pressure, consequential choices, end position |
| Relationship | Trust gained, debts incurred, betrayal, changed expectations |
| Plot | Actions that create the next problem |
| Information | What the audience and each relevant character know |
| World | Rules needed to make choices and consequences intelligible |

The point is to expose dependencies. If Episode 6 requires a betrayal, Episodes 2–5 must create a believable capacity and reason for it.

**Room question:** What must this episode inherit, and what does it make unavoidable?

**Failure to avoid:** Filling eight episode boxes with events before discovering an emotional progression.

## 2. Diagnose a stuck plot through the character’s present emotional state

**Reported principle.** In a broader craft interview, Hutchison identifies plot-driven decisions that contradict a character’s emotional position as a cause of stalled story breaking. She credits Vince Gilligan with the diagnostic question, “Where is the character’s head at?” This is a general principle from her career, not proof of a specific Rings of Power room routine. [S2]

**Our adaptation.** When a transition feels forced, stop inventing connective incidents. Write:

- What just happened to this person?
- What do they believe it means?
- What feeling are they trying to escape?
- What action would provide immediate relief?
- What does that action damage?

A character can behave irrationally while remaining emotionally legible. A frightened person might reject protection because accepting it would confirm their dependence.

**Test:** If the required plot event vanished, would they still plausibly make this choice?

## 3. Reduce epic conflict to a precise human encounter

**Reported practice.** Hutchison describes approaching Galadriel’s confrontation with Sauron through the foundation of one person betraying another and attempting to justify that betrayal. The enormous mythology grows from that intimate encounter. [S1]

**Our adaptation.** Give every major confrontation a sentence without fantasy terminology:

> Someone who needs forgiveness tries to obtain obedience instead.

Or:

> A daughter asks for permission while her father hears an announcement that she no longer needs him.

Then reintroduce institutions, artifacts, history, and spectacle.

**Why it helps:** The plain sentence identifies something actors can play and viewers can understand.

**Failure to avoid:** Believing that a council scene becomes dramatic merely because its agenda concerns the entire city.

## 4. Make the revelation work in two readings

**Reported practice.** Hutchison says Halbrand’s behaviour needed to remain coherent both before and after viewers knew his identity. She also describes revising Sauron’s language so sincerity and manipulation could coexist. [S1]

**Our adaptation: the reread table.**

| Element | First interpretation | Later interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offer of protection | Concern for another person | Creating dependence |
| Apparent confession | Willingness to be vulnerable | Selecting the truth that wins sympathy |
| Refusal of status | Humility | Avoiding scrutiny |
| Warning about an enemy | Useful intelligence | Steering attention away from oneself |

These are possibilities, not universal meanings.

**Fairness test:** The later interpretation should illuminate visible behaviour. It should not require us to invent an offscreen action that contradicts what was shown.

**Emotional test:** Whose earlier trust becomes painful after the revelation?

## 5. Change the suspense after the audience learns the secret

**Reported design.** Payne and McKay distinguish the concealed villain of Season 1 from Season 2’s situation, where viewers recognise Sauron while Celebrimbor encounters Annatar. They frame the latter relationship as a psychological thriller. [S3]

**Craft interpretation.** That changes the central experience from guessing an identity to anticipating the damage caused by unequal knowledge.

**Our adaptation.** For each secret choose deliberately:

| Audience position | Useful question |
| --- | --- |
| Knows less than the protagonist | What is this person concealing? |
| Knows approximately the same | What will we discover together? |
| Knows more | When will the character notice, and what will trust cost? |
| Knows the threat but not its limits | What can the antagonist actually do? |

Do not hide everything. Some plots become more tense when we see the danger approaching.

**Failure to avoid:** Preserving a mystery after the audience has stopped finding ignorance rewarding.

## 6. Give each storyline a distinct dramatic experience

**Reported framing.** The showrunners explicitly identify the Sauron–Celebrimbor material as psychological thriller territory. They also discuss preserving wonder and humour within a darker season. [S3]

**Our adaptation.** Assign each thread a dominant audience experience, then vary it:

| Hypothetical thread | Main experience | Necessary counterweight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Murder investigation | Suspicion and discovery | Personal attachment |
| Family succession | Loyalty and humiliation | Genuine affection |
| Assassins’ conflict | Anticipation and danger | Ordinary routines |
| Forbidden relationship | Intimacy and exposure | A shared pleasure |
| Infrastructure crisis | Dependence and urgency | Visible human consequences |

This helps an ensemble avoid sounding like six versions of the same solemn political conversation.

**Room question:** What pleasure does this thread offer that the others cannot?

## 7. Bring storylines together through incompatible objectives

**Reported design.** McKay describes Eregion’s siege as the event binding several major character threads together, with emotional scenes woven through action. [S4]

**Our adaptation.** Build a convergence table before writing a large event:

| Participant | Wants from the event | Cannot accept | Can obstruct |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Investigator | Evidence preserved | A convenient false conclusion | A political settlement |
| Heir | Public stability | Family disgrace | An honest investigation |
| Insider | Someone’s escape | Exposure of their assistance | Security measures |
| Worker | Restoration of essential services | Another sacrifice imposed from above | The elite’s timetable |

**Craft principle:** Sharing a location becomes dramatically useful when one person’s solution worsens someone else’s problem.

**Failure to avoid:** Bringing everyone to a spectacle where their earlier decisions no longer matter.

## 8. Build action in phases that change the problem

**Reported practice.** McKay describes designing the siege through distinct phases rather than one undifferentiated battle. [S4]

**Our adaptation.** A heist, riot, escape, or trial can work similarly:

1. Establish the plan and the condition required for success.
2. Remove an advantage.
3. Force a new tactic.
4. Let that tactic succeed at a cost.
5. Introduce a choice that skill alone cannot solve.

Each phase needs a changed objective, constraint, relationship, or understanding. Escalation can mean fewer options rather than more destruction.

**Example:** Entering a building unnoticed becomes extracting a witness, then deciding whether to save the witness or preserve evidence.

**Failure to avoid:** Repeated obstacles that test the same competence and leave the situation unchanged.

## 9. Draft the meaning before polishing the voice

**Reported practice.** Hutchison explains that she first wrote dialogue in plain language, then worked toward the show’s voices. She distinguishes meaningful intention from merely making Elves sound ornate. [S5]

**Our adaptation: three passes.**

| Pass | Task |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning | Say plainly what each person wants and refuses |
| Tactic | Make each line attempt something: soothe, recruit, shame, evade |
| Voice | Shape rhythm, vocabulary, silence, imagery, and social register |

**Original miniature:**

Plain meaning: “You only came because you need something.”

Refined possibility: “You remembered the address.”

The second line works only if context supplies the accusation. Removing clarity is not automatically creating subtext.

**Failure to avoid:** Polishing a weak encounter until its sentences become impressive enough to conceal the absence of movement.

## 10. Design voices through worldview and rhythm

**Reported practice.** Payne discusses studying rhetorical structures and creating different speech rhythms for Middle-earth’s peoples. Their room also regularly returned to passages from Tolkien. [S6]

**Our adaptation.** Build a voice sheet for each important individual:

- What do they notice first: price, danger, etiquette, beauty, weakness?
- Do they state a demand or make the listener infer it?
- What experiences supply their metaphors?
- When do they become unexpectedly plain?
- What do they refuse to name?

Differentiate individuals within a social group. Two aristocrats can share education while one uses precision to dominate and the other uses charm to avoid commitment.

**Exercise:** Write the same request from three characters without changing its practical meaning. Remove the names and see whether the speaker remains recognisable.

## 11. Protect ordinary life inside the epic

**Reported principle.** Hutchison discusses food, humour, comfort, and connection as grounding elements, including during terrible circumstances. Her argument is that life contains these experiences together. [S5]

**Our adaptation.** Give important relationships recurring ordinary behaviour: preparing tea, repairing clothing, splitting a meal, complaining about a shared inconvenience.

Later changes to that behaviour can carry emotion without explanation.

**Original example:** Two siblings habitually divide the last piece of bread. After a betrayal, one still divides it but leaves both pieces untouched.

The routine needs prior life and variation; otherwise it becomes an obvious symbol installed for a payoff.

**Failure to avoid:** Treating warmth as expendable because the season is dark. Viewers need access to what these people enjoy and might lose.

## 12. Let relationships create complications

**Reported emphasis.** Hutchison discusses finding common ground between apparently opposed characters and drawing on detailed relationship work. [S5]

**Our adaptation.** Track relationships as negotiated expectations:

| Question | Example answer |
| --- | --- |
| What do I receive from you? | Permission to feel competent |
| What do I give you? | Loyalty without public questions |
| What do we avoid discussing? | Who benefits more |
| What breaks the arrangement? | You demand loyalty after humiliating me |
| What survives the break? | I still know when you are frightened |

This makes a betrayal more specific than “they were close, and now they are enemies.”

**Room question:** What could only this person do to hurt—or reach—the other?

## 13. Give lore an immediate job

**Reported practice.** Hutchison describes balancing audiences with different levels of prior knowledge and using character dynamics to communicate lore. [S1]

**Our adaptation.** Introduce a world rule when it changes an available choice.

Weak delivery: a neutral lecture about how an artifact works.

Stronger situation: someone must decide whether to use it, while another person disputes the cost or conceals an exception.

Keep three categories separate:

- **Required now:** Without it, the choice makes no sense.
- **Rewarding detail:** Adds texture without blocking comprehension.
- **Future dependency:** Must be established before a later payoff.

**Failure to avoid:** Hiding an essential rule to manufacture surprise, then explaining it after it rescues a character.

## 14. Make episode endings provide both satisfaction and forward pressure

**Reported practice.** Hutchison describes the challenge of ending scenes and the season by answering a question while generating another, alongside a satisfying character movement. [S7]

**Our adaptation.** Write each episode’s ending in two clauses:

> The investigator proves the death was deliberate; the evidence now implicates the person protecting the investigation.

Or:

> The siblings reconcile; one accepts an obligation that will test the reconciliation.

A strong ending need not reveal a new villain. It can make an existing relationship impossible to continue on its old terms.

**Test:** If viewers stopped here for a week, what would feel completed? What would remain emotionally active?

## 15. Revise by problem, not by vague dissatisfaction

**Reported practice.** McKay describes extensive rewriting and a long development process. That establishes sustained iteration; it does not provide a useful numerical draft target for us. [S8]

**Our adaptation.** Name the problem before revising:

| Pass | Main question |
| --- | --- |
| Causality | Does this action produce the next situation? |
| Character | Does the choice fit the person’s present state? |
| Relationship | What changes between these people? |
| Information | Is suspense being created through clear knowledge differences? |
| World rules | Are costs and limits established before they matter? |
| Rhythm | Where do we need compression, duration, or aftermath? |
| Dialogue | Can intention survive with fewer explanations? |

Avoid changing every layer simultaneously. Keep a short decision log so a later revision does not accidentally restore a solved problem.

**Failure to avoid:** Using endless rewriting as proof that the story is improving.

## 16. Preserve the story’s identity while learning from execution

**Reported account.** McKay says Season 2 was conceived and written before Season 1 aired, while also describing learning from the experience of making the first season. That distinction matters: production learning and responding to public reception are different processes. [S3]

**Our adaptation.** After a read-through, separate:

- A problem in the premise.
- A problem in the scene’s execution.
- A missing setup.
- A taste disagreement.
- An issue created by presentation or performance.

Do not rebuild the whole season because a scene lacks clarity. Do not defend a premise problem by endlessly polishing dialogue.

## A practical workflow for our season

This is a proposed workflow, not the show’s documented schedule.

### Session 1: Define the season’s emotional promise

Complete:

> We follow ______ as they pursue ______, repeatedly choosing ______ until they must decide ______.

Write a separate sentence for the audience’s main pleasure: investigation, intimacy, power struggle, dread, adventure, or a deliberate combination.

### Session 2: Build the choices before the incidents

For every central character, identify:

1. What they initially want.
2. The compromise that appears reasonable.
3. What success costs.
4. The choice they try to postpone.
5. The final action that reveals what they have become.

A character does not need an equal number of scenes to have a coherent progression.

### Session 3: Map information and relationships

Use the templates below. Check that important revelations alter action and that relationships have more than one emotional register.

### Session 4: Give each episode its own change

Record its central crisis, dominant perspective, major decision, consequence, and ending. Let episodes vary in focus.

### Session 5: Build sustained dramatic units

List the important encounters and sequences before counting screenplay scenes. Your observation of roughly thirteen units in Episode 7 is useful as a viewing observation; it is not a verified scene count or a quota.

Ask what each unit needs to develop. A confrontation may need duration; a transition may need seconds.

### Session 6: Run a skeptical read

Challenge convenient timing, unexplained competence, forgotten resources, effortless access, implausible trust, and people withholding information solely to extend the plot.

### Session 7: Draft and read aloud

Write meaning first, then tactics, then voice. Read scenes aloud with attention to where tactics change and where listeners lose the situation.

### Session 8: Reassess the season

Check that improving an individual scene has not damaged chronology, motivation, or a later payoff. Lock decisions provisionally, with explicit reasons for reopening them.

## What we should challenge rather than imitate

**Mystery duration.** The existence of a planned answer does not mean the delay is rewarding. Test what each intervening episode adds.

**Elevated language.** A carefully researched register can still sound laboured. Keep the meaning and intention audible.

**Ensemble breadth.** Many locations can create scale while weakening attachment. Every return to a thread should earn attention.

**Spectacle.** A clearly phased action sequence can still depend on weak logistics. Check geography, resources, communication, and time.

**Convergence.** A large shared event can resolve arcs too conveniently. Preserve differences between arriving, participating, and making a decisive contribution.

**Symbolism.** Repetition is not automatically emotional accumulation. A recurring object or routine needs a changing relationship to its characters.

**Planned tragedy.** Knowledge of an ending is useful only if intermediate choices remain convincing. Avoid making everyone behave as though they know the outline.

## The five methods I would adopt first

1. Diagnose difficult plot transitions through the character’s present emotional state.
2. Draft dialogue plainly before refining tactics and voice.
3. Design revelations to change both action and the meaning of earlier scenes.
4. Make major events bring incompatible objectives into contact.
5. Give each episode a consequential change and a satisfying partial resolution.

These five offer a concrete starting discipline. The rest can support them as the season develops.
